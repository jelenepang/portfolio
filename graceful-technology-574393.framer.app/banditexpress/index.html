UI

You're a fox cop so spectacularly bad at your job that when a train gets hijacked by raccoons, someone decides you're the best person to send in. With minimal help, your mission is to sneak through a train full of trash pandas and somehow reclaim the locomotive before they make off with the railway.

Bandit Express is a 2D third-person action-puzzle game. Progressing car by car, the game blends stealth, puzzle solving, physics-based challenges, and humour as players work their way toward a final showdown with the boss raccoon.

ideation

In the first week, our team explored a variety of game concepts before settling on our final direction. Initial brainstorming included an adventure game centred around a mischievous black cat that brings bad luck to the player, requiring them to outsmart the cat and uncover its motives through environmental storytelling and shadow-based mechanics. We also considered narrative-driven concepts such as a bartender simulator, where players could influence relationships through the drinks they served, and a similar flower shop concept involving listening to customer conversations and recommending flowers to play either cupid or devil.

As we explored branching narratives and endings, concerns about its complexity and feasibility led us to pivot. Early concepts of our final direction included playing as a passenger solving a mystery or as a train staff member managing passenger satisfaction. I later proposed shifting the focus to fighting through the train to reclaim the locomotive from an antagonist. Through further team iteration, this eventually evolved into our refined game proposal.

figma ideation board

refined concept proposal

Following a greenlight review in week 3, the team refined Bandit Express into a puzzle and stealth-focused experience. Combat was removed in favour of environmental problem-solving based on a water gun that destroys cardboard boxes, which also function as platforms. Following feedback during this phase, we introduced a unique train-tilting mechanic, where train cars incline or decline as it travels over hills to create more dynamic puzzles.

Alicia focused on designing the opening levels to introduce the core mechanics and narrative, while I focused on later puzzles that built on previously learned mechanics to increase complexity gradually. To help balance progression, I labelled the mechanics used in each level to track puzzle difficulty. Around this time, I also developed the game's narrative ending.

Alongside gameplay development, we produced a Game Design Document (GDD) outlining the core mechanics, and gameplay modes. To include artistic direction in the GDD, I additionally began sketching to explore character designs and visual styles that best captured the game's playful tone.

Our proposal in a nutshell: A gang of raccoons steals a train. The solution? Send in one very unqualified fox with a personal water gun.

protoyping & playtesting

By Week 5, we had developed our first playable prototype consisting of two train cars that tested the game's core systems including player movement, shooting, raccoon patrol and chase, box destruction, and train tilting. We also implemented higher-fidelity art assets with basic idle and walking animations. Through three playtesting sessions, we identified issues with unclear objectives and player movement, prompting us to refine our game's flowboard and prioritize smoother platforming. Improvements in the raccoons movements such as the coin-luring mechanics and other animations also had to be made.

Despite this progress, the visuals were not at a stage that I was content with. Seeing all the assets together in-game and as a scene made it clear that the art direction wasn't aligning with the vision I had originally imagined. This realization highlighted the need for a stronger visual identity which ultimately led to a complete shift in the game's art style and color palette.

alpha code

During the alpha milestone, my primary focus was designing and implementing the coin-luring mechanic, which was priority given where we stood in the timeline. It was important that players could collect, carry, and drop coins to lure raccoons away from key areas and progress through the game. By using a finite-state machine, I could ensure the mechanics felt intuitive and responsive enough when integrated into the puzzle design.

Alongside this, I continued creating animations for walking and charmed raccoon states that visually communicated when raccoons were attracted to nearby coins. These additions were necessary in strengthening player feedback and made the mechanic feel more engaging during gameplay.

revisions

Following the alpha code presentation, our team continued to polish both the art and code. Cardboard friction dynamics (so boxes slide differently depending on train tilt) were improved, and automatic doors were implemented to open when the train stabilizes. We also added multiple train rotation states and started looping background art to simulate motion. Additional raccoon animations and animated cutscenes were also prepared for the beta code. Moving forward, we planned to fix issues with jump consistency pertaining to collision detection during train rotation and while hitting door triggers.

Shortly after beginning the cutscene animations, I decided to change the art style and entire visual direction.

beta code

By the beta build, substantial progress was made in both art and gameplay systems. Final elements such as player checkpoints, a restart feature, a dialogue radio tutorial system (mechanics onboarding), and cutscene transitions were added to complete the game as much as possible before the deadline. In addition to asset creation, I pushed level design a bit further especially in later parts of the game. On the code side, I was able to fix small bugs such as player jumping issues and incorrect raccoon movement. In repsonse to class feedback about repetitive train cars, I also designed more unique train car sprites at each level for visual variety and interest.

challenges

LACKING UNIQUE MECHANIC

A major iteration emerged during the “greenlight” review in week 3, when we received feedback that the game lacked a unique core mechanic. At that stage, the gameplay relied on fairly standard mechanics such as player shooting, platforming, and evading NPCs. Because the train itself did not meaningfully influence gameplay, playtests suggested that the experience was lacking and felt bland rather than exciting and unique as we had proposed. To tackle this challenge, we explored several ideas before settling on the train tilt mechanic.

UNCLEAR OBJECTIVE

Comments from participants indicated that the objective within each train car was unclear and that some mechanics were either not easily accessible or unnecessary to players. In response, we updated the functional flowboard to introduce individual tutorials for each mechanic at the start of the game and restructured gameplay so that destroying boxes became a primary mode of play. Part of solving this issue also involved redesigning the puzzles and adjusting breadcrumbing within a train car for a clearer objective.

ART REVAMP

In Week 9, feedback highlighted a lack of visual elements, which caused the game to feel short on personality compared to our initial concept proposal. In response, between Weeks 9 and 12, I made the decision to fully revamp the art direction by adopting a pixel art style. This shift to more vibrant colors and style allowed for faster iteration on visual assets and, more importantly, better supported the sense of humour we were aiming for. The change, ultimately, helped better define the game’s fun narrative and intended humorous gameplay experience.

conclusion

The development of Bandit Express was an iterative and collaborative process that evolved significantly from its initial concept. Early stages focused on ideation and defining the core direction of the game, with the team exploring multiple concepts before settling on a stealth-puzzle experience set on a moving train. As development progressed, consistent playtesting and critique sessions drove key primary mechanic decisions, including the shift away from combat towards environmental puzzle-solving and stealth. Timely critique sessions, technical milestones, and play tests allowed the team to refine core systems like player and NPC movement, train rotation, puzzle designs, and onboarding through narrative.

A key part of my contribution was deciding to revise the game’s visual direction. While early visuals established the initial world and characters, the later shift to a pixel art style and brighter color pallete brought stronger cohesion between the narrative and overall presentation. Although this required recreating all assets, the change better matched the game’s playful, light-hearted tone. The visual style also aligned more effectively with the game’s humour and intentionally “stupid protagonist” story, while improving gameplay clarity by making interactive elements and enemy states easier to recognize.


Overall, the project highlighted how interconnected all aspects of game development are. Solid core mechanics were essential before meaningful level and art polishing could occur, and feedback from playtesting continuously shaped both design and presentation. The process required constant iteration and clear communication within a small team, which ultimately led to a polished final game we are proud of. Seeing the project earn first place at the end-of-term showcase, where industry professionals played and evaluated each game, was a rewarding validation of our efforts.
